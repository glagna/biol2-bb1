The four Spotlight angles

Each team gets one angle. Fill the angle prompt + real-world tie into the slide; cite evidence from your CER tables and FL4 notebook.

TeamAngleReal-world connection
1 Tube 3 — Kanamycin (translation inhibitor). Why did green fluorescence appear but red did not? What does this prove about kanamycin's mechanism? Antibiotic resistance evolution; how translation-blocking antibiotics differ from cell-wall antibiotics, and why that distinction matters for clinical use.
2 Tube 4 — DNA B (RFP coding deletion). Why did green appear but red did not, even without kanamycin? What does this prove about gene structure? Sickle cell HBB mutation as the canonical "small change, big consequence" parallel.
3 Gene-element extension predictions. What if you removed the promoter? The start codon? The terminator? Which element matters most for whether ANY product is made? Drug discovery — designing molecules that target promoters vs. coding sequence.
4 Cross-Level connection. Translation makes proteins. Proteins like enzymes power cellular respiration (Level 1 callback). What does the BioBits result tell you about why a poison that blocks translation is so dangerous? Antibiotics, ribosomal disorders, sepsis pathophysiology.
Format guidance

You will present in 5 to 7 minutes including audience Q&A. The slide IS your visual aid — mirror it to the room's main display via Apple TV when your turn comes. No printed angle cards. No separate slides.

Cert 2 Team Oral

The final 24 minutes of the Forge Lab. 4 teams, 6 minutes each. The team being examined sits at the front; one student answers each question while the rest of the team can clarify.

Pass criteria (v9 Course Bible §5.1, verbatim)

Pass — the student can:

  • Read a 3-generation pedigree chart and determine the inheritance pattern (autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked).
  • Trace the path from a point mutation in DNA → altered mRNA → misfolded or absent protein → cellular dysfunction → systemic disease.
  • Explain the difference between genotype and phenotype, and why two people with the same mutation may have different disease severity (penetrance, environment, epigenetics).
  • Handle a variable injection: "What if this patient has one copy of the sickle cell allele instead of two? What would you expect at the molecular, cellular, and whole-body level?"
Common Not-Yet patterns
  • Can state "DNA makes protein" but cannot trace the full pathway from mutation to disease.
  • Confuses dominant and recessive inheritance.
  • Cannot read a pedigree chart.
  • Treats genetics as deterministic ("if you have the gene, you get the disease").

Not Yet is not failing. A retry path is available; retry caps at Pass.
