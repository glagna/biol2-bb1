The sickle cell trace (the IRA opener)

This trace is the single most important thing in Unit 2. You will be asked to write it on the BB2 IRA. Practice it out loud.

GAG → GTG (point mutation) in the HBB gene, position 6.
→ mRNA codon changes from GAG to GUG.
→ Amino acid at position 6 changes from glutamic acid (Glu, hydrophilic, charged) to valine (Val, hydrophobic, uncharged).
→ Hemoglobin proteins now have a sticky hydrophobic patch where they used to be soluble.
→ Under low oxygen, hemoglobin molecules polymerize (stick together in long chains).
→ Red blood cells deform from disc-shaped to sickle-shaped.
→ Sickled cells block small blood vessels (vaso-occlusion) and break apart faster than the body replaces them (anemia).
→ The patient experiences pain crises, organ damage, and chronic anemia.
LevelWhat's happening
MolecularOne base changes. One amino acid changes. One protein gains a hydrophobic patch.
CellularHemoglobin polymerizes. Red cells deform. Cells lyse early.
OrganismalVaso-occlusion. Pain crises. Organ damage. Chronic anemia.
Heterozygote variant

What if the patient has only ONE copy of the sickle allele (HbAS, "sickle cell trait")? They have BOTH normal AND sickle hemoglobin. Under everyday oxygen levels, enough normal hemoglobin keeps cells disc-shaped. Under extreme low oxygen (high altitude, intense exercise) some sickling can happen, but most carriers live without symptoms.

The trick: HbAS carriers in malaria-endemic regions have reduced severity of malaria. The Plasmodium parasite cannot grow well inside AS red cells. This is heterozygote advantage, and it explains why the sickle allele is maintained at high frequency in populations from sub-Saharan Africa, the Mediterranean, and parts of South Asia. One allele = protection. Two alleles = disease. Balancing selection is the term for this trade-off.

Stakeholder analysis (the centerpiece)

When a gene-editing decision is on the table, the question is not "is this right?" The question is "who is affected, what do they want, what do they risk, and what power do they have?"

StakeholderQuick frame
1. The CoupleWants healthy child + (in BB2) athletic enhancement. Decision-makers.
2. The Embryo / Future ChildCannot consent. Will live with the edit. Stakes are permanent.
3. The Clinical GeneticistBound by professional ethics. Has veto power on what happens in their clinic.
4. The Sickle Cell CommunityWants therapeutic edits pursued responsibly. Political and funding influence.
5. Regulatory Body (FDA)Controls whether edits can be legally performed.
6. Athletic CommissionDefines treatment vs enhancement. Can ban enhanced athletes.
7. Genome-Bot (AI advisor)Gives advice without accountability. Can be confidently wrong.

The three-column mapping: for each stakeholder, fill in WANT (what outcome they prefer), RISK (what they lose if the wrong call is made), and POWER (high / medium / low ability to shape the outcome).

This is Analyze, not Evaluate. Boss Battle 2 does NOT score your verdict. It scores the QUALITY of your stakeholder analysis. There is no "right answer." There is only a well-mapped or poorly-mapped tribunal. If you catch yourself writing "good outcome" or "bad outcome" in WANT, redo the row. Ask: good for WHOM, specifically?

The two distinctions

These distinctions are the entire frame for the Designer Baby Tribunal.

Edit typeWhat's editedInheritance
SomaticBody cells (e.g., bone marrow stem cells in Casgevy)Edit does NOT pass to children
GermlineSperm, egg, or embryo cellsEdit passes to all future generations
GoalExample
TherapeuticCorrect a known disease-causing mutation. Edit HBB to remove sickle cell.
EnhancementAugment a trait beyond typical range. Edit EPO for above-baseline athletic performance.

These distinctions stack. A single proposed edit gets BOTH labels. The four combinations:

  • Somatic + therapeutic — least controversial (Casgevy-style sickle cell treatment).
  • Somatic + enhancement — controversial (cosmetic muscle augmentation in adults; rarely done).
  • Germline + therapeutic — highly controversial (would prevent disease in descendants but raises consent issues).
  • Germline + enhancement — what He Jiankui (贺建奏) did in 2018 with the CCR5 edit. International scientific community condemned it. He was sentenced to three years in prison in 2020.

PGT vs CRISPR vs somatic gene therapy

Three tools, three different things. The single most-confused trio in Unit 2.

ToolWhat it doesModifies DNA?
PGT (preimplantation genetic testing)IVF embryos genotyped, embryos with desired genotype SELECTED for transfer.NO
Germline CRISPREmbryo's DNA modified before implantation. Affects future child + descendants.YES
Somatic gene therapyPatient's body cells modified after birth. Just the patient.YES (somatic only)

The key sentence: "PGT selects among existing embryos. CRISPR changes the DNA of an embryo or person. Somatic gene therapy changes the DNA of a patient's body cells, but the change does not pass to their children."

CRISPR-Cas9 refresher

CRISPR-Cas9 has two pieces:

  1. A guide RNA (gRNA) — a 20-letter RNA strand that matches the DNA sequence you want to cut. This is the "GPS."
  2. The Cas9 protein — a pair of molecular scissors. It binds to the gRNA, finds the matching DNA, and cuts both strands.

After Cas9 cuts, the cell has to repair the break. Two repair pathways:

  • NHEJ (non-homologous end joining) — the cell stitches the ends back together messily. Often introduces small insertions or deletions. Result: gene is broken (knockout). Fast, common, sloppy.
  • HDR (homology-directed repair) — if a template DNA is provided, the cell copies the template to repair the break. Result: gene is changed to a specific new sequence. Precise but rare; only works in dividing cells.
Real clinical example

Casgevy (FDA approved December 2023). Treats sickle cell disease by editing a different gene (BCL11A) to reactivate fetal hemoglobin in adult patients. Fetal hemoglobin doesn't sickle. NHEJ-based knockout. Patients have a one-time ex vivo edit and (in most reported cases so far) stop having pain crises.

Scope fence. You will not be asked about PAM sequences, seed-region mismatches, or specific off-target mechanisms. "Off-target risk" appears as a concept (BB2 Phase 1 1c), not a mechanism.

Pedigrees and the Punnett

A pedigree is a family tree with a job: figure out the inheritance pattern.

The two questions you ask, in order:

  1. Autosomal or X-linked? If males and females are affected at roughly the same rate, autosomal. If almost only males are affected and the trait skips generations through unaffected mothers, X-linked recessive.
  2. Dominant or recessive? If every affected person has at least one affected parent (no skipping generations), dominant. If the trait skips generations through unaffected carriers, recessive.

The Punnett square you need to know cold (HbAS × HbAS):

A (mom)S (mom)
A (dad)AAAS
S (dad)ASSS

→ 25% AA (unaffected). 50% AS (carrier, no disease in most environments). 25% SS (sickle cell disease).

Carrier vs affected: AS = carrier. SS = affected. Carriers don't have disease in everyday conditions.

Scope fence. You will not be asked Punnett questions beyond two alleles, one gene. No dihybrid crosses. No incomplete dominance. The 2 × 2 square is all you need.

Mendelian vs polygenic (the trap)

Single-gene (Mendelian) traits are caused by one gene. Sickle cell, cystic fibrosis, Huntington's. The math is clean. Punnett squares work. CRISPR can target one gene.

Polygenic traits are caused by thousands of genes, each with a small effect. Height. Skin tone. Cardiovascular disease risk. Educational attainment. Athletic performance. Depression risk.

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) sum the small effects across all those gene variants to produce a distribution-based risk estimate. PRS is a population-level statistical tool, not a personal blueprint. It can shift your risk by a small amount up or down. Useful for screening at-risk groups for cardiovascular disease. Less useful as a basis for life decisions.

The error pattern Genome-Bot will use

Genome-Bot will claim that polygenic traits are "just as editable" as monogenic ones. That you can edit one gene to produce an athletic enhancement. This is wrong. Polygenic traits don't reduce to a single gene. Editing one EPO gene won't produce a peak athlete; it will produce a person with one altered EPO gene and unpredictable downstream effects.

Catching this conflation is one of the BB2 Pass criteria.

Direct-to-consumer genetic testing (background)

23andMe-style reports tell you about your DNA, but they tell you specific things in specific ways. Read them with three filters:

  1. Relative risk vs absolute risk. "Your risk is 3x higher than average" sounds scary. If the average risk is 1 in 1,000, then 3x = 3 in 1,000 = 0.3%. Still low. Always ask: 3x WHAT baseline?
  2. Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Many genetic tests report variants that scientists don't yet know whether they affect health. A VUS is not "you have a disease" and it's not "you're fine." It's "we don't have enough data to say."
  3. Actionable vs non-actionable. A BRCA1 mutation result is actionable. There are screening and prevention options. An APOE4 result for Alzheimer's risk is much less actionable. There's no proven preventive intervention.

Privacy filter. Once you submit your DNA to a company, that company has it. Some companies share with insurers, law enforcement, or research partners. Read the privacy policy. Once your DNA is out, it's out.
